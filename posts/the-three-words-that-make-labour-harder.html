Language

The three words that make labour harder

2026-08-29 · Mark Harris

Your words in the birth room are doing one of two jobs: supporting her, or getting in her way. There is no third option.

Most of us never give it a thought until we're standing in a hospital room at 3.30am, watching the person we love climb through a tightening, with no idea what to say. So here are the three lines that make labour harder — and what to say instead.

"Just relax."

The single most useless instruction in the English language. It's a command — and her stressed mind will resist commands, because a command is a demand she has to process and possibly fail. It also implies she's doing it wrong, which is the last thing a woman in labour needs to hear. It's not that the intention's bad. It's that the sentence does the opposite of the intention.

"You're not trying hard enough."

Nobody in a birth room needs this, ever. It has been said, believe me — I've heard it. The labouring woman is doing the hardest work there is, and this sentence converts that work into a report card. It's not information, it's an accusation — and it lands in a body that's already doing everything it can.

"Is it nearly over?"

The question she doesn't need you to ask out loud. Because the honest answer is "I don't know", and "I don't know" is exactly the wrong thing to hand a woman who needs to stay in the moment. Ask the midwife instead. Then bring her the true, specific answer.

So what do you say? The good lines share one trick: they're not commands, they're anchors. Name what's verifiably true: "You can feel the bed beneath you." Offer, don't instruct: "With every breath, you might feel more at ease." Give the pain a job: "Each tightening is a wave that's bringing our baby closer to us."

And the two that carry everything else: "I'm not going anywhere." And: "You're doing this. I've got the rest."

The words you choose don't just shape the birth. They shape the story she carries away from it.
