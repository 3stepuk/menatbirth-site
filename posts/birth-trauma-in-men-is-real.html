Birth trauma in men is real

2026-08-29 · Mark Harris

Ask the man in the room how he's doing. Go on — ask him. Chances are nobody has.

More than two hundred thousand women in the UK regard their birth as traumatic every single year. We talk about that, increasingly — and rightly. What we barely talk about is the man who watched it. The partner who stood in the room while someone he loves went through the hardest thing a human body can do, without a framework for any of it.

Around one father in ten experiences postnatal depression. It doesn't always look like the textbook picture — in men it's often masked: irritability instead of sadness, withdrawal, throwing yourself into work, going quiet. And men who witness a difficult or distressing birth can carry their own marks from it — the images, the sounds, the feeling of being useless in the middle of something enormous.

There's a name in the research for one of the quieter fears: the fragility fear. The terror of hurting her. Is she healed enough, will it hurt, could I do damage. It can take the fun out of the whole business before you've started — and men carry it in silence because that's what men do.

And here's the thing I've learned in thirty years of birth work: the man in the room is not just a witness. He's a factor. The words he uses, the calm he brings, the way he speaks to her and about her — all of it changes what she carries out of that room. A huge amount of the difference between a birth that wounds and a birth that heals is made in the room, by the people in it.

So the question matters for both of you. If you're that man, and the birth was hard: say the true thing, kindly. To her, to a friend, to your GP. "That was harder than I expected" is a complete sentence, and it's a good one. The research is clear that being asked — properly, by someone who means it — changes things.

And if you're the person who knows a new dad: ask him. Not "how's the baby?" — ask him how he is, and mean it. You might be the first person who has.

Partners are not spare parts in the birth room. And they're not invisible either.
